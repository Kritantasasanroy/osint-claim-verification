# Findings report: Vitamin C supplements prevent the common cold in the general population.

**Subject type:** claim  
**Verdict:** FALSE  
**Confidence:** 88%

## Summary
Vitamin C supplementation does not prevent the common cold in the general population; it may modestly reduce cold duration and shows preventive benefit only under extreme physical stress. Major health authorities have not endorsed vitamin C for cold prevention in the general public.

## Key findings
- Regular vitamin C supplementation (≥200 mg/day) does not significantly reduce the incidence of the common cold in the general adult population. [1][2][3][4] _(confidence 92%)_
- Regular vitamin C supplementation significantly reduces the duration of cold symptoms in adults (by about 8%) and children (by about 14%). [2][5][3][4] _(confidence 90%)_
- Subgroup analyses show a preventive effect of vitamin C only in individuals under extreme physical stress (e.g., marathon runners, skiers, soldiers) with ~50% reduction in cold incidence, while no effect is seen in the general population. [2][5][3][4] _(confidence 88%)_

## Conflicting information
- No open conflict between independent sources.
  - Assessment: No conflicts detected in the provided evidence.

## Gaps and limitations
- No explicit statements or guidelines from the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, WHO, or the European Food Safety Authority regarding vitamin C for cold prevention were present in the evidence set.
- The evidence does not address long-term safety or outcomes beyond cold incidence and duration.
- There is limited information on vitamin C effects in populations with baseline deficiency or specific comorbidities outside of extreme physical stress.
