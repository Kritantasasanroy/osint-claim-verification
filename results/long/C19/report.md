# Findings report: Drinking red wine in moderation is good for heart health.

**Subject type:** claim  
**Verdict:** MIXED  
**Confidence:** 55%

## Summary
The body of evidence presents a mixed picture: moderate red wine consumption is linked to lower cardiovascular mortality in numerous observational studies and meta‑analyses, yet major health authorities caution that no causal proof exists and highlight cancer, liver disease, and addiction risks. Consequently, the claim that drinking red wine in moderation is good for heart health is assessed as mixed with approximately 55% confidence.

## Key findings
- Moderate red wine consumption is associated with a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular disease mortality and incidence compared with abstinence or other alcoholic beverages, as shown by multiple systematic reviews, meta‑analyses, and large observational studies. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8] _(confidence 88%)_
- Major health authorities, exemplified by the American Heart Association, state that no research has proven a direct cause‑and‑effect link between alcohol consumption and improved heart health and do not recommend drinking wine for health benefits. [9][9] _(confidence 94%)_
- Moderate red wine consumption carries documented risks, including increased likelihood of certain cancers, liver disease, and potential for addiction, which may offset any cardiovascular benefits. [4][7][10][11][12][13] _(confidence 82%)_
- Randomized controlled trials that adequately control for lifestyle and dietary confounders have not demonstrated a causal effect of moderate red wine intake on hard cardiovascular endpoints such as myocardial infarction or cardiovascular death. [14][15][16] _(confidence 70%)_
- Specific polyphenol components of red wine (e.g., resveratrol, quercetin, catechins) can improve cardiovascular biomarkers and endothelial function, and these compounds are obtainable from non‑alcoholic food sources. [17][8][18][19][20][21][22] _(confidence 78%)_

## Conflicting information
- No open conflict between independent sources.
  - Assessment: While multiple observational studies and meta-analyses associate moderate red wine consumption with reduced cardiovascular mortality, major health authorities such as the American Heart Association state that no research has proven a direct cause‑and‑effect link between alcohol intake and improved heart health, creating a tension between epidemiological associations and causal evidence.

## Gaps and limitations
- Long‑term randomized controlled trials that isolate the effect of moderate red wine consumption from other lifestyle factors are lacking, limiting causal inference.
- Most evidence on benefit comes from observational studies susceptible to residual confounding (e.g., diet, socioeconomic status, exercise).
- Data comparing the magnitude of cancer and other disease risks to cardiovascular benefits in integrated population models are sparse and often rely on assumptions.
- Evidence for the clinical relevance of red wine polyphenols when consumed without alcohol is limited to short‑term biomarker studies; hard outcome data are missing.
