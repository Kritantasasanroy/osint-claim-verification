# Findings report: Vitamin C supplements prevent the common cold in the general population.

**Subject type:** claim  
**Verdict:** FALSE  
**Confidence:** 88%

## Summary
The claim that vitamin C supplements prevent the common cold in the general population is false. High‑quality Cochrane evidence shows no reduction in cold incidence with regular supplementation, though there is a modest decrease in symptom duration and a preventive effect limited to individuals undergoing extreme physical stress. Major health authorities have not issued explicit endorsements for vitamin C as a cold preventive in the general populace.

## Key findings
- Regular vitamin C supplementation (≥200 mg/day) does not significantly reduce the incidence of the common cold in the general adult population. [1][2][3][4] _(confidence 93%)_
- Regular vitamin C supplementation produces a modest but statistically significant reduction in cold duration (≈8% in adults, ≈14% in children). [2][5][3][4] _(confidence 88%)_
- Subgroup analyses indicate a preventive effect of vitamin C only in individuals under extreme physical stress (e.g., marathon runners, skiers, soldiers), with no effect in the overall general population. [2][5][3][4] _(confidence 86%)_

## Conflicting information
- No open conflict between independent sources.

## Gaps and limitations
- No explicit statements or recommendations from the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, WHO, or EFSA regarding vitamin C for cold prevention in the general population were located in the evidence set.
- The evidence does not address very high-dose vitamin C regimens (>2 g/day) or long-term safety outcomes relevant to routine supplementation.
