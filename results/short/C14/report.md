# Findings report: Coffee consumption is associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes.

**Subject type:** claim  
**Verdict:** TRUE  
**Confidence:** 85%

## Summary
The available evidence indicates that regular coffee consumption is associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes, supporting a TRUE verdict with approximately 85% confidence.

## Key findings
- The most recent meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies reports a pooled relative risk of 0.71 (95% CI 0.67-0.76) for high versus low coffee consumption. [1][2][3][4][5] _(confidence 92%)_
- After adjusting for age, BMI, smoking, and physical activity, coffee consumption remains significantly associated with lower type 2 diabetes risk (confidence intervals exclude 1). [6][3] _(confidence 85%)_
- No high-quality prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials in the reviewed literature reported a null or positive association between coffee consumption and type 2 diabetes risk. [6][2][5][4] _(confidence 78%)_
- Major health authorities such as the American Heart Association and the Mayo Clinic state that coffee consumption is linked to a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes. [7][8] _(confidence 80%)_
- The reviewed sources do not provide a GRADE or similar certainty rating for the evidence linking coffee consumption to type 2 diabetes risk. [1][2][6][5][4][3] _(confidence 60%)_
- No randomized controlled trials examining coffee intake and incident type 2 diabetes were identified in the available sources. [1][2][6][5][4][3] _(confidence 55%)_
- Subgroup analyses by sex, ethnicity, or baseline BMI were not reported in the reviewed sources, so heterogeneity of effect across subgroups remains unknown. [1][2][6][5][4][3] _(confidence 50%)_

## Conflicting information
- No open conflict between independent sources.

## Gaps and limitations
- No explicit GRADE assessment of certainty was found in the surveyed literature.
- The evidence base lacks randomized controlled trials specifically designed to test coffee's effect on incident type 2 diabetes.
- Subgroup analyses (sex, ethnicity, baseline BMI) are absent, limiting understanding of effect modification.
- Potential publication bias or small-study effects were not evaluated in the provided sources.
- Details on dosage‑response shape beyond broad categories (e.g., exact cups per day) are limited in some sources.
